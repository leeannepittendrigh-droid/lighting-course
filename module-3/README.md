## Lesson 1: National Electrical Code (NEC) :id=lesson-1

<details class="clc-lesson-details" open>
<summary class="clc-lesson-summary">Show / hide this lesson</summary>

### What Is the NEC?

The **National Electrical Code (NFPA 70)** is published by the National Fire Protection Association and sets standards for safe electrical installation in the United States.

- Updated every 3 years (2020, 2023, 2026, etc.)
- Adopted by most U.S. states and municipalities, sometimes with local amendments
- Covers wiring methods, equipment installation, and safety requirements
- Enforced by local **Authorities Having Jurisdiction (AHJs)**

**Why it matters:** All lighting installations must comply with the NEC. Inspectors verify compliance before issuing permits and certificates of occupancy.

### NEC Articles Relevant to Lighting

The NEC is organized into "Articles." Here are the key ones for lighting:

| Article | Topic | Key Content |
|---|---|---|
| 90 | Introduction | Purpose and scope of the NEC |
| 100 | Definitions | Key terms used throughout |
| 110 | General Requirements | Installation conditions, clearances |
| 210 | Branch Circuits | Circuit requirements for lighting |
| 220 | Calculations | Lighting load calculations |
| 250 | Grounding | Grounding requirements for fixtures |
| 400 | Flexible Cords | When cords are permitted |
| **410** | **Luminaires** | **Main article for lighting fixtures — your primary reference** |
| 700 | Emergency Systems | Emergency lighting requirements |
| 701 | Standby Systems | Legally required standby power |

---

### Article 410: Key Requirements for Fixtures

**410.10 — Luminaires in Specific Locations:**
- Fixtures in wet locations must be marked "Suitable for Wet Locations"
- Fixtures in damp locations must be marked "Suitable for Damp Locations"
- Outdoor fixtures must be wet-rated
- Fixtures under covered areas may be damp-rated — check local interpretation
- Corrosive locations require special fixtures that resist the specific chemicals present

**410.16 — Clearances:**
- Fixtures must maintain required clearances from combustible materials
- Recessed fixtures must maintain the clearances specified on the fixture label — typically 1/2 inch from combustible materials
- **IC-rated** fixtures can be in direct contact with insulation
- **Non-IC** fixtures must have 3-inch clearance from insulation

**410.36 — Personnel Protection:** Exposed live parts must be prevented from accidental contact, and fixtures must be installed to prevent mechanical damage.

**410.62 — Cord-Connected Fixtures:** The cord must be visible for its full length, strain relief is required, and there are maximum length restrictions and specific permitted cord types.

**410.116 — Clearances for Recessed Fixtures:** Minimum 1/2 inch from combustible materials, with thermal protection required. Less critical for LED than older lamp types, but the housing requirements still apply.

**410.130 — Installation of Track Lighting:** Load limitations, support requirements, connection methods, and restrictions on where track lighting can be installed (e.g., closets, above bathtubs).

---

### NEC Voltage Classifications

Understanding voltage classes is important for product selection:

- **Class I (0–600V):** Includes all standard commercial lighting voltages — 120V, 208V, 240V, 277V, 480V
- **Low Voltage (under 50V):** Includes 12V and 24V systems (LED tape, some accent lighting) — different wiring rules apply
- **Line Voltage (120–277V most common):** Direct connection to building power

---

### Grounding Requirements (Article 250)

All metal fixtures must be grounded for safety.

**Grounding methods:** Equipment grounding conductor (green wire or bare copper), metal raceways (if continuous and properly bonded), or cable armor (if approved for grounding).

**Why grounding matters:** Prevents shock hazards, provides a path for fault current, trips breakers in case of a short circuit, and is required for UL listing compliance.

---

### Working Space and Clearances (Article 110)

Electricians need safe working space around electrical equipment: minimum 30 inches wide, minimum 36 inches deep (in most cases), minimum 6.5 feet high, and free of obstructions.

> ⚠️ **Why this matters:** Don't place fixtures where they block access to electrical panels or equipment.

---

### Labeling and Marking Requirements

The NEC requires fixtures to be labeled with: manufacturer name or trademark, voltage rating, wattage or current rating, suitability for wet/damp locations (if applicable), maximum lamp wattage (if applicable), and lamp type (if applicable).

> Look for the **UL or ETL label** — this indicates compliance with NEC requirements.

## Lesson 2: NFPA 101 Life Safety Code :id=lesson-2

<details class="clc-lesson-details">
<summary class="clc-lesson-summary">Show / hide this lesson</summary>

### What Is NFPA 101?

NFPA 101, the **Life Safety Code**, addresses life safety from fire and similar emergencies. It covers means of egress, emergency lighting, exit signs, fire alarm integration, and occupancy-specific requirements. It's adopted by most states and municipalities, enforced by fire marshals and building inspectors, and required for building occupancy permits.

---

### Emergency Lighting Requirements

**Who needs it:** Almost all commercial, industrial, institutional, and assembly buildings — office buildings, retail stores, warehouses and factories, schools and universities, hospitals and healthcare facilities, hotels and multifamily residential, restaurants and bars, and places of assembly (theaters, churches). Very small tenant spaces (under 500 sq ft) and some residential occupancies may be exempt — check local requirements.

**NFPA 101 Section 7.9.2.1 — Minimum Illumination:**
- Average illumination: **minimum 1 foot-candle** (10.8 lux) along the path of egress
- Minimum at any point: **0.1 foot-candle** (1.1 lux)
- Uniformity ratio: average-to-minimum not to exceed **40:1**

In plain terms: the average light level must be at least 1 fc, even the darkest spot must have at least 0.1 fc, and you can't have one bright area and one very dark area.

**Where these levels must be maintained:** Exit doors and doorways, corridors and passageways, stairs and ramps, exit discharges (the area immediately outside exit doors), and any portion of the egress path.

---

### 90-Minute Runtime Requirement

NFPA 101 Section 7.9.2.3: emergency lighting must operate for a **minimum of 90 minutes** when normal power fails — enough time for orderly evacuation, for emergency responders to arrive and operate, and for safe facility shutdown if needed.

- Battery capacity must maintain required illumination levels for the full 90 minutes — can't dim below required levels as the battery depletes
- Must be tested annually to verify (90-minute discharge test)

**Automatic operation:** Emergency lighting must activate automatically **within 10 seconds** of power failure, require no manual intervention, and operate independently of normal lighting switches. Most emergency systems actually transfer in under 1 second — the 10-second window is the maximum permitted.

---

### Exit Sign Requirements

NFPA 101 Section 7.10 — marking of means of egress:

- Every exit must be marked with an approved exit sign, visible from any direction of approach
- Minimum letter height: **6 inches** for new construction (older buildings may be grandfathered with smaller letters)
- Maximum viewing distance: **100 feet**
- Must be continuously illuminated or self-luminous, and maintained during power failure (battery backup or connected to emergency power)
- Red or green lettering permitted — green is becoming more common as the international standard, and colors must contrast with the background

---

### Testing and Maintenance Requirements

NFPA 101 Section 7.9.3:

| Test | Frequency | Duration |
|---|---|---|
| Function test | Monthly | 30 seconds |
| Full discharge test | Annual | 90 minutes |

The monthly test verifies lights activate; the annual test verifies batteries maintain required illumination for the full duration and documents initial illumination levels.

> Written records of all tests must be maintained and available for inspection. Modern emergency lighting can include self-testing features that automatically perform monthly and annual tests and log results.

---

### Battery Backup vs. Generator

| Method | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|
| **Battery backup** (most common) | Simple, no fuel required, minimal maintenance; individual battery packs in or near fixtures, or inverter systems powering regular fixtures | Limited to 90-minute runtime |
| **Generator backup** | Can run indefinitely with fuel; may supplement battery systems | Higher upfront cost, requires testing and maintenance of the generator |

NFPA 101 permits either method — battery backup is most common for egress lighting due to its simplicity and cost.

---

### Occupancy-Specific Requirements

Different occupancy types have additional requirements:

- **Assembly occupancies** (theaters, churches, arenas): more stringent requirements, aisle lighting required, special provisions for large spaces
- **Healthcare occupancies** (hospitals, nursing homes): critical areas need hospital-grade systems, additional requirements for patient care areas, often require generator backup
- **High-rise buildings:** stricter requirements, often require generator backup, stairwell lighting is critical

Always verify specific requirements for the occupancy type you're working with.

## Lesson 3: Energy Codes :id=lesson-3

<details class="clc-lesson-details">
<summary class="clc-lesson-summary">Show / hide this lesson</summary>

### Overview

Energy codes set **maximum limits on watts per square foot** for lighting — think of it like a fuel economy standard for buildings (a minimum mpg requirement, but for lighting it's a maximum watts requirement).

**Why energy codes matter:** Building officials won't issue permits for non-compliant designs. You can't specify any fixture — it must meet efficiency requirements. Many rebates require energy code compliance, and a non-compliant design discovered after installation is an expensive fix.

**Major U.S. energy codes:**
1. **ASHRAE 90.1** — Energy Standard for Buildings Except Low-Rise Residential Buildings
2. **IECC** — International Energy Conservation Code
3. **Title 24** — California Energy Code (most stringent in the U.S.)

Most states adopt one of these codes, often with modifications — your project must comply with whichever code your state or city has adopted.

> 💡 **Beginner Tip:** Your first step on any project is finding out which energy code applies. This determines your design limits.

---

### Understanding Lighting Power Density (LPD)

**LPD** is the amount of watts you're allowed to use per square foot of floor area — the primary way energy codes limit lighting energy use. Think of it like a speed limit: you cannot exceed it without violating code.

**Formula:** `LPD = Total Watts ÷ Total Square Feet`

**Example:** A 10,000 sq ft office using 9,000 watts of total fixture wattage → LPD = 9,000 ÷ 10,000 = **0.90 W/sq ft**. If the code allows 0.98 W/sq ft for offices, this design complies. ✅

**Why it matters:** LPD determines how many fixtures you can install. Exceed the limit and you'll face code violations and project rejection.

### ASHRAE 90.1 LPD Limits by Space Type

| Space Type | Max W/sq ft | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Office | 0.82–0.98 | Standard task lighting needs |
| Retail | 1.20–1.59 | Higher — accent lighting needs |
| Warehouse | 0.43–0.63 | Lower — high ceilings, less activity |
| Classroom | 1.05–1.24 | Higher — reading, detailed work |
| Parking garage | 0.19–0.27 | Lowest — basic safety lighting only |

**Practical impact:** A 10,000 sq ft office at a 0.90 W/sq ft limit allows a maximum of 9,000W total. Using 40W fixtures, that's **225 fixtures maximum**. Need more fixtures? Specify lower-wattage (higher-efficacy) fixtures instead.

---

### Two Compliance Methods

ASHRAE 90.1 gives you two ways to prove compliance — pick whichever works better for your project.

**Building Area Method (simpler):** One LPD limit applied to the entire building based on its type. Simple to calculate, but one-size-fits-all can be restrictive.
*Example:* A 50,000 sq ft office building at 0.90 W/sq ft → maximum 45,000W total.

**Space-by-Space Method (more flexible):** Different LPD limits for each space based on actual use — usually allows more total watts, but requires documenting every space.
*Example, same 50,000 sq ft building:* 40,000 sq ft offices @ 0.90 = 36,000W, 5,000 sq ft conference @ 1.23 = 6,150W, 5,000 sq ft corridors @ 0.66 = 3,300W → **45,450W total allowed** — 450W more than the Building Area Method.

> 💡 **Beginner Tip:** Most designers use Space-by-Space because it usually allows more watts. The trade-off is more paperwork.

---

### Mandatory Lighting Controls (ASHRAE 90.1)

- **Automatic shutoff** — required in all buildings, via occupancy sensors, time scheduling, or manual-on with automatic shutoff (typically 20–30 minute time limits in most spaces)
- **Space control** — each space must have independent control; large areas generally can't be switched together
- **Multi-level control** — ability to reduce lighting power by at least 50% in most spaces, via dimming, bilevel switching, or a combination
- **Daylight responsive controls** — required in daylight zones (within 15 feet of windows or under skylights), automatically dimming or turning off lights when daylight is available

**Exterior lighting** has its own LPD limits based on application and lighting zone (Zone 0 = undeveloped/most restrictive through Zone 4 = high-activity city centers/most permissive), plus control requirements: automatic shutoff (astronomical time clock or photocell) and a 30–50% reduction after hours, depending on zone.

---

### IECC — International Energy Conservation Code

Published by the International Code Council (ICC). Similar to ASHRAE 90.1 but with some different values — uses the same LPD concept, offers both Building Area and Space-by-Space methods, and has similar control requirements (automatic shutoff, multi-level control, daylight controls). States typically adopt either IECC or ASHRAE 90.1, not both, though some create hybrid codes combining elements of each.

---

### Title 24 — California Energy Code

California has the **most stringent energy code in the U.S.**

**Why it matters outside California:** Many manufacturers design to Title 24 compliance, other states often adopt similar requirements, and projects may need Title 24 for LEED or other certifications.

**Key requirements beyond ASHRAE 90.1:**
- **Lighting Power Density** — more restrictive than ASHRAE/IECC in most cases
- **Occupancy sensing** — required in virtually all spaces, with specific control strategies by space type (vacancy vs. occupancy mode)
- **Daylight controls** — automatic daylight dimming in all daylight zones, more stringent than ASHRAE 90.1
- **Partial-off controls** — ability to turn off lights in specific zones, supporting task tuning
- **Demand response** — capability to reduce lighting power by at least 15% on a demand signal
- **High-efficacy requirements** — minimum efficacy requirements for most fixture types, eliminating low-efficiency products
- **Certification** — fixtures must be certified Title 24 compliant and listed in the California Energy Commission database

---

### Compliance Documentation Required

| Document | What It Contains |
|---|---|
| Lighting Plans | Fixture schedule, watts per fixture, control zones, switch locations |
| LPD Calculations | Documented calculations showing compliance, space-by-space breakdown if applicable |
| Controls Narrative | Description of control strategies and how code requirements are met |
| Product Data | Spec sheets, control device specs, certifications (DLC, Title 24, etc.) |
| Commissioning | Controls testing and verification records |

## Lesson 4: Safety Certifications — UL & ETL :id=lesson-4

<details class="clc-lesson-details">
<summary class="clc-lesson-summary">Show / hide this lesson</summary>

### Why Safety Certifications Matter

Safety certifications verify that products meet specific safety standards. In the U.S., UL or ETL listing is typically required for commercial lighting installations. Without proper certification: the electrical inspector can reject the installation, insurance may not cover damages from uncertified products, you can't obtain a certificate of occupancy, and there's liability exposure for the installer and property owner.

---

### UL (Underwriters Laboratories)

An independent safety science company, founded in 1894, that tests and certifies products for safety — the most recognized safety certification in North America. UL testing covers electrical safety (shock, fire hazards), mechanical construction, flammability of materials, performance under normal and fault conditions, environmental exposure, and marking/labeling requirements.

**UL marks you'll see on lighting:**
- **UL Listed** — the full product has been tested; most common for complete fixtures
- **UL Recognized** — a component has been tested (like a driver); must be incorporated into a UL Listed product, can't be used alone
- **UL Classified** — tested for a specific use or condition (e.g., "Suitable for Wet Locations")

---

### ETL (Intertek)

ETL stands for "Electrical Testing Laboratories," now owned by Intertek, a global testing company. ETL tests products to the same standards as UL and is equally accepted by inspectors and code officials.

**ETL vs. UL:** Both test to the same UL standards (e.g., UL 1598 for lighting fixtures) and are both Nationally Recognized Testing Laboratories (NRTLs). ETL is typically faster and less expensive for manufacturers; some customers prefer UL for name recognition, but both are legally equivalent.

> **Important:** Inspectors must accept either UL or ETL — they cannot require a specific lab.

---

### Key UL Standards for Lighting

| Standard | Application |
|---|---|
| UL 1598 | Luminaires (general-use lighting fixtures) |
| UL 153 | Portable Electric Lamps |
| UL 1573 | Stage and Studio Lighting |
| UL 1838 | Low-Voltage Landscape Lighting |
| **UL 924** | **Emergency Lighting** (exit signs, emergency lights, battery packs) |
| UL 8750 | LED Equipment |
| UL 2108 | Low Voltage Lighting Systems |

> ⚠️ Emergency lighting products **must be UL 924 listed** to comply with NFPA 101.

---

### Wet and Damp Location Ratings

- **Suitable for Dry Locations** — indoor use only, protected from weather (standard office, retail, etc.)
- **Suitable for Damp Locations** — protected from direct water exposure; examples: covered outdoor areas, bathrooms, some parking garages; can withstand high humidity and condensation
- **Suitable for Wet Locations** — direct exposure to water; examples: fully exposed outdoor, car wash, hose-down areas; must be IP65 or higher

> ⚠️ **Critical:** The fixture must be marked for the location where it's installed. Using a damp-rated fixture in a wet location violates code.

---

### Other Certifications on Spec Sheets

| Certification | Notes |
|---|---|
| **FCC Part 15** | Required for all LED fixtures — limits electromagnetic interference (EMI) and prevents radio/TV interference. Class A (commercial) or Class B (residential, more stringent). |
| **CE Mark** | Required for European markets. Not required in the U.S., but indicates compliance with EU directives. |
| **RoHS** | Restriction of Hazardous Substances — environmental regulation limiting hazardous materials. Required for the EU, increasingly required in U.S. states (California Prop 65 relates to this). |
| **CSA** | Canadian Standards Association — the Canadian equivalent of UL, required for Canadian installations. Many fixtures are UL/CSA dual-listed. |

## Lesson 5: DLC & ENERGY STAR :id=lesson-5

<details class="clc-lesson-details">
<summary class="clc-lesson-summary">Show / hide this lesson</summary>

### Understanding the DLC (DesignLights Consortium)

A non-profit organization that maintains the **Qualified Products List (QPL)**, focused on commercial LED lighting quality and performance — the most important certification for rebates.

**Why the DLC exists:** Utility rebate programs needed a standardized way to verify LED product quality. The DLC provides that verification.

### DLC Tiers

| Tier | Requirements | Rebate Impact |
|---|---|---|
| **DLC Standard** (formerly "DLC Listed") | Meets baseline performance requirements, minimum efficacy thresholds, 5-year warranty required, basic quality standards | Standard rebates |
| **DLC Premium** | Higher efficacy requirements (10–20% higher than Standard), stricter quality requirements | **Highest rebate tiers** ✅ — most rebate programs strongly prefer or require Premium |

**Example efficacy requirements (2024):**

| Fixture Type | DLC Standard | DLC Premium |
|---|---|---|
| High Bays | 110 lm/W min | 130 lm/W min |
| Troffers | 105 lm/W min | 125 lm/W min |
| Area Lights | 100 lm/W min | 120 lm/W min |

*(Requirements update periodically — always verify current specs at designlights.org.)*

**What DLC testing covers:**
- **Performance** — efficacy (lm/W), power factor (≥ 0.90), THD (≤ 20%), CCT accuracy, CRI (≥ 70–80 depending on product), zonal lumens
- **Quality** — L70 rated life (minimum 36,000 hours), operating temperature range, warranty (minimum 5 years)
- **Testing requirements** — third-party testing to LM-79 (photometric performance), LM-80 data (LED lumen maintenance), TM-21 projections (L70 calculations)
- **Reporting** — detailed spec sheets in standardized format, IES files, product photos

---

### Typical Rebate Structure

| Tier | Typical Rebate Range |
|---|---|
| DLC Standard | $20–40 per fixture |
| DLC Premium | $40–80 per fixture |

**Example — warehouse project, 100 high bays:** DLC Standard rebate: $30/fixture = $3,000. DLC Premium rebate: $60/fixture = $6,000. **Difference: $3,000.**

> 💡 **Bottom line:** Always specify DLC Premium when possible. The incremental product cost is usually far less than the additional rebate.

---

### The DLC Qualified Products List (QPL)

An online database of all DLC-qualified products at **[www.designlights.org](https://www.designlights.org)**.

**How to use it:** Search by category (High Bays, Troffers, etc.), filter by tier (Standard vs. Premium) and by manufacturer, view specs for each product, and verify model numbers before quoting.

**Why use it:** Verify products qualify for rebates, compare efficacy and specs across brands, confirm current listing status (products can be removed if quality issues arise), and download spec sheets.

> ⚠️ Model numbers must match **exactly**. Even small variations may not be listed.

---

### DLC Product Categories

**Currently qualified:** High/Low Bay Fixtures | Linear Fixtures | Troffer/Recessed Fixtures | Retrofit Kits | Outdoor Area/Roadway Fixtures | Outdoor Wall-Mounted Fixtures | Canopy/Soffit Fixtures | Parking Garage Fixtures | Horticultural Lighting (newer category)

**Not covered:** Decorative/Architectural fixtures (no performance-based standards) | Residential products | Most Downlights | Track Lighting | Emergency Lighting (performance varies in emergency mode)

---

### ENERGY STAR vs. DLC

| Program | Focus | Best For |
|---|---|---|
| **ENERGY STAR** (EPA program) | Broader products, residential focus | Residential and some commercial products |
| **DLC** | Commercial lighting only | **Commercial projects** ✅ |

ENERGY STAR matters most for residential-style commercial products, downlights and recessed fixtures not covered by DLC, some retrofit lamps, and decorative fixtures. For most commercial projects, **DLC is more important than ENERGY STAR** — though some products carry both certifications.

## Lesson 6: IP & IK Ratings :id=lesson-6

<details class="clc-lesson-details">
<summary class="clc-lesson-summary">Show / hide this lesson</summary>

### IP Rating (Ingress Protection)

**Format:** IP + two digits (e.g., IP65)

**First digit — solid/dust protection (0–6):**

| Rating | Protection |
|---|---|
| 0 | No protection |
| 2 | Finger-sized objects |
| 4 | Wire-sized objects |
| 5 | Dust protected |
| 6 | Dust tight |

**Second digit — liquid protection (0–9):**

| Rating | Protection |
|---|---|
| 0 | No protection |
| 4 | Splash-resistant |
| 5 | Water jet resistant |
| 6 | Powerful water jet |
| 7 | Temporary immersion |
| 8 | Continuous submersion |

**Common IP ratings:**

| Rating | Protection Level | Typical Use |
|---|---|---|
| IP20 | Basic indoor | Indoor only |
| IP44 | Splash-resistant | Some covered outdoor |
| IP54 | Dust + splash | Covered outdoor |
| **IP65** | **Dust tight, water jet** | **Most outdoor fixtures** ✅ |
| IP66 | Dust tight, powerful jet | Canopy, harsh weather |
| IP67 | Dust tight, immersion | Ground-level, wet areas |
| IP68 | Dust tight, submersion | Underwater, pools |

---

### IK Rating (Impact Protection)

**Format:** IK + two digits (e.g., IK08)

| Rating | Impact Energy | Typical Use |
|---|---|---|
| IK07 | 2 joules | Indoor, low impact |
| **IK08** | **5 joules** | **Standard outdoor** ✅ |
| IK09 | 10 joules | High-impact areas |
| IK10 | 20 joules | Vandal-resistant |

**When IK matters:** IK08 is the standard for outdoor commercial applications (parking lots, wall packs). IK10 is for high-vandalism areas, sports facilities, ground-level fixtures, and gas stations.
